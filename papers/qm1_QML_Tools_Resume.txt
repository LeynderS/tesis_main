Quantum Support Vector Machines. QSVM builds upon the traditional Support Vector
Machine (SVM) framework by incorporating quantum computing principles. Classically, SVMs
operate by identifying a hyperplane in a high-dimensional feature space that effectively separates
different data classes. The optimal hyperplane maximizes the distance, or margin, from the nearest
points of each class, known as support vectors.
In contrast, the QSVM avoids the computationally intensive task of constructing a high-
dimensional feature space by using a quantum circuit to embed data into a quantum feature
space. This process, known as the quantum feature map, utilizes the parallel processing capabil-
ities of quantum computers. The embedded states, denoted as |𝜙(⃖⃗𝑥)⟩, reside in a Hilbert space
of exponentially large dimension, enabling expressive representations that are often inaccessible
classically.
Within this quantum feature space, the QSVM computes kernel functions, essential elements
in SVMs that assess the similarity between pairs of data points. In quantum computing, these
kernel functions are linked to the concept of fidelity, a measure that evaluates the similarity or
“distance” between quantum states represented as state vectors or density matrices. Fidelity ranges
from 0 (completely distinguishable states) to 1 (identical states). The quantum kernel is defined
mathematically as

and interference. This reduces the memory and computational overhead associated with traditional
kernel methods and opens up the possibility of solving sorting problems that would otherwise be
inaccessible to classical algorithms.

Although QSVMs offer the potential for exponential gains, their performance is sensitive to noise
and errors in today’s quantum hardware. Kernel estimation is especially vulnerable: small gate or
decoherence errors can distort fidelity values, producing unreliable or even non-positive semidefinite
kernel matrices. Moreover, in high-dimensional quantum spaces, most pairs of states are nearly
orthogonal, which can cause all kernel values to converge to zero and render classification inefficient.
To address this, several mitigation techniques are employed. One approach is to restrict the
expressivity of the quantum feature map, thereby limiting the exploration of the full Hilbert space
and preventing the generation of nearly orthogonal quantum states. Another strategy involves
scaling the input data, which increases the average overlap between encoded states and improves the
stability of kernel estimation. Additionally, projected or structured kernels can be used to simplify
state comparisons by focusing on specific observables or subspaces, thus reducing sensitivity to
noise and enhancing the discriminative power of the kernel matrix.
While the QSVM promises enhanced speed and efficiency for complex, high-dimensional classifi-
cation tasks, its practical application remains challenging due to limitations in current quantum
technology, such as qubit availability and quantum errors. Realizing the full potential of QSVM
depends on advances in quantum computing, particularly in quantum error correction, an ongoing
area of intense research.
Furthermore, there exist other versions of QSVM, like the PegasosQSVM. The PegasosQSVM
algorithm is basically the same as QSVM, except that it is adapted for larger datasets and implements
the Pegasus (Proximal Gradient Descent for Sparse Learning) algorithm to handle regularization
terms that induce sparsity in solutions. This adaptation allows PegasosQSVM to be more efficient
and scalable, especially in contexts where data is massive and sparsity is desired to improve
interpretability and reduce model overfitting. There are also other types of kernels, which can be

6.1 Existing Frameworks
Various frameworks and libraries enrich the landscape of quantum computing, each offering
distinctive features and capabilities for developing and executing quantum algorithms. Given the
variety of quantum computing frameworks available, it is natural to consider which one is the
most suitable for beginners. Each framework has its own strengths and weaknesses, which can
significantly impact the learning experience and the types of projects you may want to pursue.
In this section, we provide an overview of several prominent quantum computing frameworks
and libraries, including Qiskit, Pennylane, Q#, Cirq, Wolfram Quantum, Amazon Braket, Strawberry
Fields, TensorFlow Quantum, and OpenFermion. We discuss each framework’s architecture, features,
and suitability for various quantum computing tasks. Additionally, we highlight the advantages
and limitations of each framework to assist researchers and practitioners in selecting the most
appropriate tool for their specific needs.
Qiskit [48], developed by IBM, is an open-source quantum computing framework that provides
tools for quantum circuit construction, simulation, and execution, with a user-friendly programming
interface and extensive documentation. Qiskit supports hybrid quantum-classical computing and
integrates with classical machine learning libraries, though it may face scalability and resource
utilization limitations.

6.2 Integration of QML with Cloud-based Quantum Computing Platforms
In the last years, cloud-accessible quantum computers have grown from experimental prototypes to
fully managed services capable of running millions of circuit evaluations per day. Public platforms

offer researchers immediate and uniform access to superconducting, trapped ion and neutral atom

6.3 Quantum Datasets
Quantum datasets provide a consistent basis for evaluating and comparing quantum algorithms and
hybrid models. However, preparing quantum-compatible datasets is often complex, as it requires
either preprocessing classical data or generating data through simulations or quantum hardware.
A notable example is QDataSet [88], which offers a scalable framework for converting classical
datasets into quantum circuit representations. Each dataset includes Qiskit-compatible circuits
that encode data using methods such as angle or amplitude encoding, along with standardized
splits, labels, and metadata describing the encoding process. This structure supports reproducibility
and simplifies benchmarking by allowing researchers to focus on model design rather than data
preparation. QDataSet covers a range of machine learning tasks, including classification, regression,
and generative modeling, using both classical benchmarks (e.g., Iris, MNIST) and synthetic datasets
tailored to quantum applications.
The Pennylane Quantum Data module [87] offers a collection of datasets designed for use in
QML workflows. Examples include the moon dataset for binary classification and several molecular
datasets intended for quantum chemistry simulations using variational algorithms. Although
relatively small, these datasets are practical for experimentation, benchmarking, and instructional
use within hybrid quantum-classical settings.
The Open Quantum Data Commons (OpenQDC) [79] is a large-scale repository of quantum
mechanics datasets. It includes nearly 40 datasets covering over 1.5 billion molecular geometries
across 70 atomic species and 250 quantum methods. Each dataset is standardized and enriched with
metadata and is accessible through a Python API that supports efficient formats.
